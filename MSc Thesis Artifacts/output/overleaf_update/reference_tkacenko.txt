Abstract
Increasing air traffic places growing pressure on airlines and airports to operate efficiently,
with delayed turnarounds representing a major cost factor. While process mining can
support the analysis and improvement of business processes, aircraft turnarounds lack
the detailed event logs required for such analysis. Existing studies frequently rely on
aggregated, case-centric milestones that do not capture the interactions among the op-
erational resources involved in the process. To bridge this operational data gap, we
generate synthetic event logs through simulation. We combine domain knowledge and
limited empirical data in a colored Petri net model of the turnaround process. The
model represents process dependencies and interactions among multiple objects. Monte
Carlo simulation generates candidate traces, which are exported as object-centric event
logs (OCEL) 2.0. Evaluation against a limited ground-truth dataset shows partial agree-
ment in internal activity durations. Although obtaining sufficient valid traces requires
numerous simulation iterations, fusing domain knowledge with limited empirical data
successfully yields synthetic OCEL 2.0, providing a foundation for object-centric process
mining.

1 Introduction
Within Europe alone, the aviation industry registered 11.1 million flights in 2025, with
traffic continuing to rise [26]. As air traffic grows, delays incur significant losses by
decreasing aircraft usability and creating extra costs such as non-productive fuel con-
sumption, labor costs, and passenger compensation [35]. Up to 70% of these delays are
related to turnaround (TA) processes [35]. The TA is defined as the critical period be-
tween an aircraft’s arrival at the gate (in-block) and its subsequent departure (off-block).
It involves a complex sequence of parallel and sequential processes, including refueling,
catering, and boarding. While airlines and airports face pressure to optimize TA dura-
tions, the process remains an operational black box. Since TAs are complex business
processes, formal process modeling is required to understand the underlying resource
interactions. Process mining (PM) has proven powerful in creating such operational
transparency [29] but its application is hindered by a fundamental lack of data. Because
of the manual and fragmented nature of ground operations, detailed event logs are not
readily available to enable standard PM techniques. Such event logs are, however, essen-
tial to develop targeted algorithms capable of systematically improving operations and
handling error-prone data from diverse sources.
For bridging this data gap, we propose a simulation-based approach for reconstruct-
ing process traces using colored Petri nets (CPNs) to extract object-centric event logs
(OCEL) 2.0. Unlike related methods relying on traditional case-centric paradigms,
OCEL 2.0 explicitly captures object-to-object relationships, relationship qualifiers, and
evolving attribute values [9]. This enables an explicit representation of resource lifecycles
and their interactions with TA instances. Our CPN model is built incpn-py[8], a scal-
able, Python-based framework that offers a low entrance barrier and seamless integration
with modern PM environments.
To capture the inherent variability of ground operations, we generate candidate traces
through Monte Carlo simulation. The evaluation examines completion and routing prop-
erties of selected model configurations, compares simulated activity durations with lim-
ited ground-truth observations, and assesses the OCEL 2.0 against relational validation
checks.
The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows. Section 1.1 motivates the need
for object-centric TA simulations, followed by the problem statement in Section 1.2.
Section 1.3 and Section 1.4 define the overarching research questions and specific goals.
Finally, Section 1.5 lists the main contributions of this thesis.

1.1 Motivation
The aviation industry faces high average delays, causing costs of approximately€1.5
billion in 2019 [13]. Up to 45% of these delays are reactive and caused by delay propa-
gation [33]. A large proportion of these delays originate at hub airports like Frankfurt
Airport (FRA) [35], which function as critical transit points. To optimize the TA and
thereby reduce delays, a detailed analysis of the as-is state is required to identify dis-
ruptions and bottlenecks.
Existing PM approaches often rely on airport collaborative decision making (A-CDM)
data. While A-CDM provides high-level timestamps (e.g., in-block and boarding start),
they are partly recorded manually, which yields impossible process variants and related
analytical shortcomings [29]. Our approach prevents this by utilizing a CPN simula-
tion, whose structural constraints restrict generated traces to behavior permitted by the
modeled control-flow and resource constraints.
While some existing approaches utilize Petri Nets to model the TA, they do not
provide event logs to enable PM. Furthermore, if one were to apply PM to these models,
they would be limited by a case-centric paradigm. Traditional PM treats a single flight
as the isolated moving part. The TA, however, is an interdependent process involving
multiple distinct resources, such as ground support equipment and specialized crews.
For instance, consider a catering truck and a cleaning crew that must coordinate access
to the cabin. In a traditional case-centric event log, the independent lifecycle of the
cleaning crew, which serves multiple different aircraft across the apron, is lost. This
leads to convergence and divergence issues, where a single event is related to different
cases, or multiple instances of the same activity occur within a single case [6]. This
motivates an representation that links TA instances and resources.
To solve this, our method generates object-centric event data by extracting OCEL 2.0
from a CPN simulation. OCEL 2.0 captures object-to-object relationships, relationship
qualifiers, and evolving object attributes. Returning to the previous example, our ap-
proach models both the aircraft and the catering truck as independent objects, explicitly
representing their modeled interactions and resource dependencies. By combining CPN
simulation with object-centric process mining (OCPM), we reconstruct missing process
traces, transforming the black-box process into a transparent, analyzable event log.

1.2 Problem Statement
1.2 Problem Statement
High-quality, detailed event logs for aircraft TAs are currently unavailable due to the
decentralized and manual nature of the ground handling processes. Existing simulation
approaches can represent shared-resource dependencies. The challenge addressed here
is to expose these modeled interactions as structured object-centric event data for sub-
sequent analysis. Thus, a method is missing to reconstruct realistic, OCEL 2.0 based
on limited available real-world data required for a comprehensive, data-driven process
analysis.
1.3 Research Questions
Building on these challenges, we define the following Research Questions (RQ) to guide
our approach:
RQ1: How can aircraft TA activities and their complex resource dependencies be formally
reconstructed based on sparse real-world data and domain knowledge?
RQ2: How can simulation techniques be applied to this model to create OCEL 2.0?
RQ3: How can the quality of these reconstructed event logs be validated against limited
ground truth data?
Together, these questions formulate a comprehensive methodology to synthesize, recon-
struct, and evaluate object-centric event logs.
1.4 Research Goals
Reconstructing TA traces from sparse observations requires a synthesis of formal model-
ing and process simulation. Therefore, to systematically address the previously defined
research questions, the following research goals are established:
1. Systematically capture and formalize aircraft TA processes and their complex re-
source dependencies into an executable CPN.
2. Develop a Monte Carlo simulation approach to approximate missing TA activities
by generating plausible process traces as valid OCEL 2.0.
3. Validate the generated OCEL 2.0 against limited ground truth data.
Achieving these goals yields synthetic event logs whose temporal plausibility is assessed
against limited empirical data. This allows advanced process mining techniques to sys-
tematically optimize aircraft TA operations.

2.2 Timed Colored Petri Nets
We model the operational behavior and the manipulation of object states using timed
CPNs [16], hereafter referred to as CPNs. To do so, we first define essential concepts.
Definition 2.2.1(Multisets and Timed Multisets).LetB(S) be the set of all multisets
over a setS. Given the universe of timestampsU time introduced in Definition 2.1.1, a
timed multiset overSis a multiset over the Cartesian productS×U time. An element
(s,τ)∈S×Utime is written ass@τ, indicating that the elementsbecomes available at
timeτ.
Definition 2.2.2(Variables and Expressions).LetVbe a set of typed variables. We
define the set of all expressions that can be evaluated using variables fromVasEXPR V .
The type of an expressionexp∈EXPRV is denoted byType(exp). For expressions not
containing any free variables, we use EXPR∅.
Definition 2.2.3(Timed Colored Petri Net).A timed CPN is a nine-tuple
N= (P,T,F,Σ,V,C,G,E,I), where:
1.Pis a finite set of places.
2.Tis a finite set of transitions such thatP∩T=∅.
3.F⊆(P×T)∪(T×P) is a set of directed arcs.
4. Σ is a finite set of non-empty color sets. A color set can be either untimed or
timed.
5.Vis a finite set of typed variables such thatType(v)∈Σ for allv∈V.
6.C∈P→Σ is a color set function that assigns a color set to each place. A place
pis timed ifC(p) is timed, otherwise,pis untimed.
7.G∈T→EXPRV is a guard function that assigns a boolean expression to each
transitiont∈Tsuch thatType(G(t)) =Bool.
8.E∈F→EXPRV is an arc expression function that assigns an expression to each
arcf∈F. For any arcfconnected to a placep, the following holds:
•Type(E(f)) =B(C(p)) ifpis untimed.
•Type(E(f)) =B(C(p)×U time) ifpis timed.

sion to each placep∈Psuch that:
•Type(I(p)) =B(C(p)) ifpis untimed.
•Type(I(p)) =B(C(p)×U time) ifpis timed.
The initial markingM 0 is a state defined over the places of the CPN. It is obtained by
evaluating the initialization expressions for all places, i.e.,M 0(p) =I(p) for allp∈P.

2.3 Mapping OCEL 2.0 Concepts to CPN Semantics
Having defined OCEL 2.0 for data representation and CPNs for operational modeling,
we now describe the mapping between them.
Conceptually, an object token combines an object identifier with its current attribute
values. Let
Cobj =U obj×(Uattr̸→Uval)
denote this abstract representation, where the partial mapping contains the attributes
applicable to the respective object type. The concrete CPN model implements this
representation through typed product color sets, which are introduced in Section 4.1.2.
When an event modifies an object attribute, this is represented by the occurrence
of a transitiont∈T. The attribute update is formalized using the function update
operator⊕. Letc= (o,π)∈Cobj represent the current state of objecto, whereπis its
attribute mapping. If a transition changes attributeoatov new, the resulting abstract
token state is
c′= (o,π⊕{(oa,vnew)}),
where⊕denotes function update. In the concrete model, the same operation is im-
plemented by replacing the corresponding component of the typed token tuple. This
produces a new token holding the updated attribute values, integrating object-centric
attribute changes into the execution semantics of the CPN.
2.4 Monte Carlo Simulation
To capture the stochastic nature of real-world processes, we evaluate the dynamic be-
havior of our CPN model through Monte Carlo simulation [27].
Definition 2.4.1(Monte Carlo Simulation).Monte Carlo simulation is a numerical
method for the quantitative analysis of stochastic systems. It estimates the behavior
of a model through repeated evaluations, using input parameters randomly sampled
from predefined probability distributions. Grounded in the Law of Large Numbers,
this method ensures that the average of the simulated outcomes converges to the true
expected value as the number of independent trials increases.
Monte Carlo simulation can be combined with rejection sampling: candidate samples
are generated and retained only if they satisfy a specified acceptance criterion.
2.5 Conclusion
In summary, this chapter establishes the theoretical foundation for our approach. We
formalize OCEL 2.0, define CPNs as our operational model, and describe their concep-
tual mapping. Finally, we introduce Monte Carlo simulation as the quantitative method
for evaluating stochastic system behavior.

3 Related Work
This chapter contextualizes the proposed methodology by reviewing the existing litera-
ture. First, we examine approaches to simulating ground operations. Subsequently, we
discuss applications of PM in the aviation sector, highlighting limitations in the avail-
ability and granularity of event data. Finally, we explore the emerging shift towards
object-centric paradigms.
3.1 Modeling and Simulating Aircraft TAs
Traditionally, aircraft TAs have been scheduled and analyzed as linear projects using the
critical path method (CPM). CPM calculates the minimum project duration by isolat-
ing the critical path, the longest sequence of zero-buffer tasks where any delay extends
the entire process, thereby pinpointing bottlenecks. These models, however, typically as-
sume deterministic process times and static resource availability, treating parallel ground
activities in isolation rather than capturing their dynamic interdependencies [22].
To systematically assess the complex and uncertain nature of TAs, Schmidt [33] high-
lights the need for flexible, fine-grained simulation approaches. Several authors have
utilized advanced Petri nets to represent the concurrency of apron activities and re-
source constraints. Vidosavljevic and Tosic [35] deploy CPNs to investigate resource
allocation and schedule disruptions, using daily operational plans and available equip-
ment as inputs. Sng [34] combines Petri nets with Monte Carlo simulation to identify
bottlenecks and evaluate process modifications. The model is calibrated using opera-
tional records, supplemented by observations, aircraft manuals, and expert input where
empirical data is unavailable. Furthermore, Cui [12] combines time transition Petri nets
with Bayesian updating to dynamically predict TA times under operational uncertainty.
These works demonstrate the suitability of Petri nets for representing concurrency,
synchronization constraints, and stochastic activity durations in ground handling oper-
ations. Their focus is on performance evaluation, resource allocation, and turnaround-
time prediction. Building on these capabilities, our approach uses CPN simulation to
generate candidate traces and systematically maps their events, objects, and relation-
ships on OCEL 2.0 for subsequent object-centric process analysis.

Recent research applies PM to improve transparency and analyze operational perfor-
mance in aviation. B¨ ohm et al. [11] describe the introduction of commercial PM soft-
ware at Lufthansa CityLine to support punctuality and reliability in ground operations

Rott et al. [29] apply PM to A-CDM milestone data from Munich Airport, revealing
performance differences and data quality issues. Inaccurate or inconsistent timestamps
can produce implausible recorded sequences, such as going off-block before boarding has
concluded. The analysis therefore depends on the availability, granularity, and quality
of the recorded milestones. Our approach instead uses IBT and OBT as temporal an-
chors, supplemented by domain knowledge, activity-duration distributions, and resource
assumptions, to generate candidate executions without requiring a A-CDM event log.
The CPN constrains process variants that violate the encoded business rules.
3.3 Object-Centric Paradigms
Linear process descriptions for the TA often fail to accurately reflect TA realities because
they ignore the complex interactions between shared resources across the apron. De L´ ıger
et al. [19] emphasize that TA operations must handle the synchronization of competing
resources, a complexity that models such as CPMs cannot manage.
To accurately capture these dynamics, it is necessary to adopt an interacting object-
centric paradigm, where resources and aircraft are treated as independent yet tightly
coupled entities. Luo et al. [20] demonstrate the value of this approach by utilizing a
digital twin to represent aircraft and ground support equipment as distinct, interacting
entities rather than treating the TA linearly as a single flight case. Their results confirm
that modeling the autonomous interactions of multiple resources yields a more accurate
reflection of ground handling dynamics. Building on the utility of such simulations,
another study by Luo et al. [21] successfully leveraged generative modeling. By creating
a synthetic dataset of 30,000 TA records to train predictive machine learning models,
they validated that simulating ground operations is a robust methodological approach
to overcome the limitations of incomplete empirical datasets.
Collectively, these studies demonstrate the value of modeling interacting resources and
generating synthetic operational data through simulation. Our approach builds on these
principles by mapping CPN transition firings and token values to events, objects, quali-
fied relationships, and object attribute changes in OCEL 2.0. This makes the simulated
interactions between aircraft, jobs, and resources explicitly accessible for object-centric
process analysis.

This chapter introduces an approach for reconstructing candidate TA traces. The core

high-level constraints, such as in-block time (IBT) and off-block time (OBT), and basic

4.1.1 Hierarchical Architecture
We address the complexity of the aircraft TA using a top-down hierarchical modeling
strategy [5]. Hierarchical design follows a divide-and-conquer paradigm, decomposing a
complex system into manageable modules to abstract detailed logic at higher levels [17].
Guided by Occam’s razor (the principle that “one should not increase, beyond what is
necessary, the number of entities required to explain anything”) the simulation model
is designed to be as simple as possible while retaining the process dependencies con-
sidered essential within the scope of this study [3]. The modular structure supports
model maintenance and the reuse of subprocess components. Since hierarchical CPNs
do not increase formal expressive power, any hierarchical model can be unfolded into an
equivalent non-hierarchical CPN, thereby satisfying our definition ofN TA [17].
Following an incremental refinement approach [17], we initially modeled the abstract
TA sequence. Subsequently, we iteratively detailed logically connected modules, such as
cabin operations, baggage and cargo handling, followed by more independent processes
like water servicing. After each module integration, the model was visually inspected
and its execution behavior was checked against the adopted domain rules.
At the highest level of abstraction, the top-level module establishes the structural
backbone of the TA, capturing the overarching process from thein-blockto theoff-block
event (see Figure 4.2). The fine-grained operational dynamics of each subprocess are

4.1.3 Business Rules
Business rules impose resource, concurrency, and sequential constraints on the TA pro-

of an activity, an available resource is claimed and assigned the TA identifier. Upon
completion, the resource is released and its assignment cleared. This explicit assignment
enables the extraction of object-to-object relations between resources and aircraft for
OCEL 2.0.

4.1.4 Stochastic Timing
Once the base CPN is constructed and the resources are integrated, the time perspective
is introduced, following the recommendations of Jensen [16]. To avoid the pitfall of
assuming constant durations [4], the duration of each event is modeled using probability
distributions. Consequently, for each simulated turnaround, the activity duration of an
operational transition is sampled from its respective distribution. Figure 4.3 illustrates,
withboardingas an example, that each activity consists of astartand anendtransition.
Thestarttransition samples the duration delay, while the zero-delayendtransition fires
immediately after this duration concludes, facilitating the subsequent extraction of the
event log.
Activity durations are dependent on specific operational factors. For instance, LCC
generally exhibit shorter process times than FSC [33], and the presence of PRM impacts
boarding and deboarding durations [15]. Accordingly, the transition activity duration
is determined by the respective token attributes. The sampled delay is defined asd∼
Da(cAC), whereD a is the probability distribution for a given taska, parameterized by
the attributes of the aircraft tokenc AC.
For our FRA-focused proof of concept, we combine general FSC event durations from
Asadi et al. [7] with comparable LCC data from Schiphol Airport [20]. Additionally, the
expert interview revealed that “almost every aircraft carries a PRM” [14].

4.4 Conclusion
This chapter outlined a threefold pipeline designed to synthesize OCEL 2.0 from limited
boundary data. The process begins with the formalization of TA operations in a hier-
archical CPN, integrating domain knowledge, business rules, and stochastic timing to
represent the assumed process structure and stochastic timing behavior. In the second
phase, a Monte Carlo simulation generates a pool of candidate traces, utilizing rejection
sampling to enforce temporal validity against real-world data. Concurrently, the final
phase employs a custom extraction engine that intercepts state changes and token flows,
mapping them into an OCEL 2.0 database. This approach provides an implementation
for generating synthetic object-centric event data.

5.2.2 Early Pruning
The efficiency of the simulation relies on the search strategy used to map tokens to
variables. Below, we discuss the limitations of the original depth-first approach and
demonstrate how applying early pruning to partial bindings resolves severe memory and
runtime bottlenecks.
State Space Explosion in Token BindingThe originalBacktrackBindingalgorithm
utilizes a recursive depth-first search to systematically bind tokens to variables. How-
ever, it operates without intermediate filtering. It recursively builds complete dictionary
combinations, only evaluating the transition’s guard once a full binding is reached at
a leaf node of the search tree. Returning to themerge servicesexample in Figure 5.1:
assuming five tokens per input place, the unmodified code sequentially constructs and
evaluates these full combinations. In a worst-case scenario, it must traverse all 625 per-
mutations. In large-scale scenarios with numerous tokens, this late evaluation causes a
combinatorial explosion, leading to severe computational overhead.
The Early Pruning MechanismTo resolve this computational bottleneck, the search
architecture was rebuilt using a two-step approach. First, the execution mechanism
was modified to continuously evaluate the intermediate state (partial binding) at ev-
ery step of the backtracking process. Second, utilizing the partial-safe callable guards
introduced earlier,cpn-pycan verify conditions on incomplete dictionaries immediately.

7.1 Methodological Design Choices
This section discusses the main methodological choices underlying the proposed pipeline,
including the selection of input data, the representation of jobs and resources, the inte-
gration of CPN simulation with OCEL 2.0, and the use of Monte Carlo simulation to
generate temporally plausible synthetic event data.
Data SelectionThe overarching goal of our approach is to generate TA event data
from sparse observations without relying on frameworks like A-CDM. As discussed in
Chapter 3, relying on A-CDM for PM exposes vulnerabilities: it lacks the granularity
required for detailed analyses, remains prone to manual entry errors and case-centricity,
and limits generalizability. The decision to bypass this framework and instead build a
trace reconstruction pipeline based on universally available milestones (IBT and OBT)
reduces dependence on a specific input format and remains a system-agnostic approach.
Modeling and Data ExtractionTo achieve fine-grained process reconstruction, we
chose CPNs as our modeling formalism. They provide the expressiveness required to
represent complex, concurrent processes, such as aircraft TAs, as emphasized in Sec-
tion 1.1. A CPN simulation serves as an executable specification and takes aforward-
lookingperspective, generating possible process executions under assumed conditions [3].
PM complements this perspective through thebackward-lookinganalysis of recorded ex-
ecutions [2]. To enable this analysis for the simulated behavior, we extract the token
flow into an object-centric representation based on OCEL 2.0. This representation cap-
tures dynamic attribute changes and explicit relationships between events, resources,
and TA instances, making shared-resource assignments observable across multiple TAs.
As demonstrated by the what-if analysis, these relationships support the investigation
of resource contention and concurrency alongside their timing implications. Since the
analyzed executions are synthetic, the findings describe the modeled system; interpreting
them as evidence about actual airport operations requires further empirical validation.

7 Discussion
Job and Resource TypingA generic job color set simplifies model maintenance: in
our implementation, changes to the aircraft color set before simulation initialization are
automatically reflected in the corresponding job structures. Similarly, a generic resource
color set provided flexibility during model development, when resource assignments were
not yet fully specified. While sufficient for this proof of concept, it groups heterogeneous
resources under a single object type in the exported OCEL 2.0. Resource categories
remain distinguishable through attribute filtering, but are not directly represented as
separate object types. Future implementations should therefore use dedicated color sets
for distinct resource types, with properties tailored to each type, to support more explicit
object-centric analysis.
Simulation via Monte CarloBecause of the stochastic nature of the simulation, single
simulation runs rarely match the temporal boundary between IBT and OBT exactly. A
naive linear rescaling of event durations would align the simulation with actual Off-Block
Times, but this would distort variances and invalidate the empirical data’s integrity.
Alternatively, Bayesian Inference could compute posterior event durations based on the
observed total TA time [23, 36]. However, TAs rely on synchronization points where
progression is dictated by themaximumduration of parallel paths. These lack a closed-
form solution for conditional probabilities of non-normal random variables [28] and would
make the model too complex to scale.
Therefore, our approach of choice is the Monte Carlo method with rejection sampling.
It preserves the stochastic integrity of the model while providing enough plausible traces
within the temporal boundaries through a high number of iterations. This approach
entails high computational costs, as reflected in the 85.82% rejection rate in Chapter 6.
However, performance optimizations forcpn-py, such as callable guard functions and
early pruning introduced in Chapter 5, make this approach feasible.
Value of Synthetic DataGiven the scarcity of detailed event data, the proposed recon-
struction uses IBT and OBT as temporal anchors, supplemented by domain knowledge,
activity-duration distributions, and resource assumptions. Building on the simulation
approaches in Chapter 3 [7, 34], it generates candidate executions for unobserved ac-
tivities between these boundaries. The resulting object-centric data support the de-
velopment and testing of analytical workflows without comprehensive empirical event
logs.
The evaluation nevertheless shows that agreement with the overall TA duration does
not imply agreement for individual activities. Observed fueling and catering durations
frequently fall within the simulated intervals, whereas other activities exhibit systematic
deviations. Accepted traces therefore represent possible executions consistent with the
modeled constraints and temporal acceptance criterion, without establishing accurate
reconstruction of internal behavior. Their value lies in supporting object-centric analysis
and exposing discrepancies that warrant further investigation of model assumptions. No
parameters were tuned to the small ground-truth sample, reducing the risk of tailoring
the model to these observations while leaving the discrepancies unresolved.

7.2 Practical Applicability
7.2 Practical Applicability
Highlighting the value of the proposed pipeline, we detail its low-barrier accessibility, its
role as a bridging technology, and its capacity for predictive what-if analyses.
Accessible Process AnalysisUnlike PM applications based on A-CDM data, which de-
mand organizational integration, the proposed pipeline offers an accessible, low-barrier
entry point for TA analysis. Because it relies on data points that are universally avail-
able via airport databases, airline schedules, or public APIs such as the OpenSky Net-
work [32], airlines and ground handlers can readily reconstruct fine-granular process
traces. While the underlying stochastic distributions and resource availabilities must be
calibrated to local airport characteristics to ensure maximum accuracy, the methodology
offers a potential basis for operational analysis, subject to local calibration and further
evaluation.
Bridging TechnologyStrategically, this synthetic data reconstruction acts as a bridg-
ing technology. It allows stakeholders to develop and test PM workflows, such as perfor-
mance dashboards, without data being available yet. Once high-resolution, automated
tracking technologies (e.g., with computer vision or IoT apron sensors) become univer-
sally deployed in the future, the analytical workflow can subsequently be adapted to
empirical event data, provided that event and object semantics are aligned.
What-If analysesBeyond retrospective diagnosis, the pipeline supports predictivewhat-
ifanalyses, as demonstrated in Section 6.4. By manipulating the parameters and re-
source constraints within the CPN model, such as simulating the availability of an addi-
tional baggage loader, airports, airlines, and ground handlers can prospectively evaluate
different optimization strategies. This gives decision-makers a method to quantify the
impact of infrastructural changes on the overall TA duration and resilience.
7.3 Extensibility and Generalizability
Beyond the initial proof of concept, the following evaluates the pipeline’s structural
extensibility and its potential application to other industries.
7.3.1 Extensibility
As a proof of concept, this approach focuses on a case study at FRA, modeling one apron
and one building position for narrow-body aircraft. The proposed method is designed
to be scalable and the pipeline is best suited for specific positions, where the typical
durations of ground operations are well-documented. Although the current simulation
is tailored to the specific operational constraints of FRA, the pipeline can be adapted
to other airports, provided that the In-Block and Off-Block Times of the TAs, as well
as the duration distributions for the respective ground operations, are available.
43

7 Discussion
While scaling the model increases computational demands and execution times, the
underlying CPN architecture allows for comprehensive structural extensions. As de-
tailed in Chapter 5, the simulation can be adapted to incorporate the physical apron
topology, expand to additional aircraft and positions, increase subprocess granularity, or
integrate entirely new subprocess modules. The current day-wise initialization captures
shared-resource dependencies between TAs within each operational day. Extending the
simulation across multiple days would additionally require preserving ongoing activi-
ties and resource states across day boundaries and adapting time-dependent guards and
duration functions where applicable.
7.3.2 Generalizability
Beyond aviation ground operations, the underlying methodology extends to a broad
spectrum of business processes. The combination of CPN, Monte Carlo simulation, and
OCEL 2.0 may be transferable to other domains facing a similar “black box” problem.
This requires three core prerequisites:
•Knowledge of the logical process structure and resource dependencies.
•Empirical or estimated probability distributions for individual activity durations.
•Reliable boundary timestamps indicating (at least) the start and end of the overall
process instance.
To apply this methodology, domain experts first translate the process logic, business
rules, and involved object types into a CPN model. After configuring the stochastic
duration parameters, the Monte Carlo simulation creates a pool of process variants.
The extraction engine then transforms these simulated interactions into OCEL 2.0. By
applying boundary timestamps as an acceptance window, TA-specific candidates satis-
fying the temporal acceptance criterion are retained for analysis. As demonstrated in
our evaluation in Section 6.2, this pipeline should initially be validated against a subset
of manually recorded GT data to confirm that the pipeline approximates reality.
This synthetic OCEL 2.0 recreation is particularly valuable for manual and concur-
rent manufacturing environments, such as final assembly lines. Similar to an aircraft
TA, these assembly processes are bounded by start and end times, involve multiple
interacting resources (e.g., mechanics, specialized tools, components), and often lack
granular, sensor-based tracking during execution. By bridging this data gap, the pro-
posed approach enables OCPM in domains that previously lacked the necessary event
data infrastructure.
7.4 Limitations
While the proposed approach successfully recreates process traces, several limitations
regarding the pipeline must be acknowledged:
44

7.5 Conclusion
•Underrepresentation of Edge Cases:Stochastic simulations based on prob-
ability distributions excel at representing standard operations. However, they
structurally struggle to replicate anomalies, such as technical breakdowns, medical
emergencies, or extreme weather events, if they are not explicitly modeled.
•Context Blindness:Currently, multivariate dependencies are abstracted into
general probability distributions. In reality, activity durations correlate with dy-
namic external factors like weather conditions or workforce schedules. Integrating
these would require higher context-awareness within the CPN model.
•Distribution Decay:The empirical plausibility of the generated OCEL 2.0 re-
lies heavily on the quality of input durations. Structural changes at an airport,
such as new aircraft types or altered handling procedures, render the underlying
distributions outdated and necessitate re-calibration.
•Evaluation Constraints:Finally, we did not perform quantitative conformance
checking (e.g., measuring fitness and precision) [31]. This decision stems from
the scarcity of high-resolution, publicly available event logs in the aviation sector.
Evaluating the pipeline against our single dataset would provide limited evidence.
•Internal Trace Validation:While the pipeline adheres to the overall temporal
boundaries, this primarily demonstrates that the simulation can be conditioned
on the total TA duration. It does not inherently guarantee that the duration of
internal activities are correctly reconstructed. Accurately calibrating the internal
mechanics of the TA process requires further expert validation and the integration
of reliable intermediate milestones.
7.5 Conclusion
In conclusion, the proposed methodology demonstrates how the integration of CPNs,
Monte Carlo simulation, and the OCEL 2.0 data model can help illuminate the op-
erational black box of aircraft TAs. By reducing dependence on frameworks such as
A-CDM, this approach provides a pathway to OCPM based on boundary timestamps,
domain knowledge, and stochastic process parameters. While the reliance on synthetic
data requires careful calibration and limits conclusions about actual operations, the
framework offers practical value for developing analytical workflows and exploring re-
source interactions throughwhat-ifanalyses. It also serves as a bridging technology,
enabling the development and testing of process mining applications before detailed
empirical event data become available.
The modular architecture supports extensions to additional subprocesses, resource
types, and operational settings. However, the challenges of distribution decay, context
blindness, and incomplete relational export conformity must be acknowledged. The
implementation demonstrates feasibility for the investigated case study, while computa-
tional scalability and transferability require further evaluation. Beyond aviation ground

Addressing how turnaround activities and their resource dependencies can be formally
reconstructed based on sparse data (RQ1), we developed a trace reconstruction pipeline
that synthesizes qualitative domain knowledge with quantitative temporal boundaries
and stochastic activity durations. The configurable CPN model captures the process

To translate this conceptual model into object-centric event data (RQ2), we executed
a Monte Carlo simulation with temporal acceptance filtering. This allowed us to select
candidate traces consistent with the observed turnaround durations and extract simu-

SQLite database supports downstream process analysis.

limited empirical data (RQ3), we compared simulated activity durations with a sparse
ground-truth dataset. The analysis revealed that while temporally accepted traces were
successfully generated for reference turnarounds, agreement varied across internal ac-
tivities. These findings highlight the dependence of the reconstruction on the assumed
duration distributions and show that agreement with the overall turnaround duration
alone does not establish accurate reconstruction of its internal execution.
Beyond this empirical assessment, the what-if analysis demonstrates how the gener-
ated object relationships support the investigation of resource sharing and concurrent
assignments across turnarounds. It illustrates the pipeline’s practical use for exploring
resource configurations and their timing implications within the modeled system.
Finally, we outlined how the modular pipeline can be extended to additional subpro-
cesses, resources, and operational settings. The proposed trace reconstruction methodol-
ogy may also be transferable to other domains characterized by manual processes lacking
automated event tracking, provided that reliable temporal boundaries, suitable process
parameters, and sufficient domain knowledge are available. Computational scalability
and transferability require further evaluation.

8.1 Future Work
This thesis presents a viable trace reconstruction pipeline and highlights several promis-
ing areas for further investigation. We recommend subsequent work to focus on empiri-
cally validating the event logs, refining the CPN model, enhancing the usability through
artificial intelligence, and exploring real-time applications.
Evaluation and ValidationFirst, future research could benefit from prioritizing more
extensive evaluations using larger ground truth datasets to assess the pipeline’s accu-
racy. Once empirical event data becomes available for specific positions (e.g., through
advanced tracking systems likeseer), formal conformance checking techniques [30] can
be employed. By calculating metrics such as fitness and precision, structural deviations
between the generative model and real-world operations can be uncovered.
Since the airport-wide deployment of hardware-intensive tracking systems remains a
long-term industry goal, it does not obsolete the need for advanced trace reconstruction.
Building on the demonstrated what-if analysis, future work could investigate a broader
range of resource configurations and assess the sensitivity of the results to duration
distributions and allocation rules. Finally, validating these synthetic OCEL 2.0 against
defined object-centric declarative constraints [18] will ensure that all reconstructed traces
adhere to domain rules and cross-object interactions.
Model Refinements and ScalabilityTo increase the fidelity of the simulation, the CPN
model can be enhanced. Currently, the choice for resources of which aircraft to serve next
is random. Future iterations could introduce priority-based token routing, ensuring, for
example, that an aircraft that has already received catering is prioritized by the cleaning
crew. Additionally, the static duration distributions could be transitioned to dynamic,
machine-learning-driven parameters. By employing regression models or context-aware
neural networks, exact task durations could be predicted per token based on complex
feature sets (e.g., weather conditions, aircraft type, or exact passenger loads). Finally,
conducting a comprehensive case study with an airport operator using the pipeline would
validate the scalability of the approach in a real-world setting.
Usability and large language model (LLM) IntegrationTo increase the accessibility
of the pipeline, integrating LLMs offers significant potential. LLM support could act as
an interface to simplify structural changes to the process model, allowing domain experts
to adjust it without Python knowledge. Furthermore, LLMs could automatically infer

interactions into semantically rich event-to-object relationship types.

API data (e.g., real-time trajectories from the OpenSky Network [32]) into the simula-

A Expert Interview Transcript
This appendix presents an English translation of the German transcript of the expert
interview on turnaround operations conducted on 6 February 2026.
Interview date:6 February 2026
Interviewee:Expert in turnaround operations at Fraport AG
Speaker labels:I = Interviewer; E = Expert
Thesis Context and Problem Statement
I:This interview is being recorded exclusively for scientific purposes, and participation
is voluntary. Do you consent to the recording and the use of the data as described in
the consent document?
E:Yes.
I:To briefly explain the background of my bachelor’s thesis: the main objective is to
combine different data sources to build a simulation of the turnaround process that is
as accurate as possible. Individual sources are available, such as timestamps from the
A-CDM framework or GPS data for determining positions. My goal is to represent these
data in a colored Petri net . One advantage is its mathematical precision in representing
resource dependencies. The simulation is intended to generate event logs for process
mining, for example to identify bottlenecks. This is still uncommon in turnaround oper-
ations because data are missing and activities such as baggage handling are performed
manually. The simulation is intended to address this gap.
Realism of Manufacturer Manuals
I:My current data sources include aircraft manuals, such as the A320 Aircraft Charac-
teristics / Airport and Maintenance Planning document, as well as location data. For
passenger handling, this document assumes a boarding rate of 12 passengers per minute
for 180 passengers. This would imply a boarding duration of 15 minutes. How realistic
are such figures?
E:The figures in these documents are highly idealized. They assume that two sets of
stairs are used, one at the front and one at the rear. Low-cost carriers place considerable
emphasis on short turnarounds, and they do mostly board passengers using two sets
of stairs simultaneously. Such a rate is more achievable under those conditions. For a
51

A Expert Interview Transcript
network airline such as Lufthansa at a contact stand, usually only one passenger boarding
bridge is connected at the front. Under these circumstances, 15 minutes is unrealistically
fast.
It also depends on the definition: does boarding begin when a passenger enters the
aircraft, or already at the gate? At a remote stand, passengers travel to the aircraft
by bus. Even if two sets of stairs are available at the aircraft and the rate is briefly
achieved, there are waiting times of five to six minutes between buses.
Minimum Turnaround Time and Process Boundaries
I:Do you use these manuals in practice?
E:We rarely use manufacturer manuals in day-to-day operations. What we do use is the
Minimum Turnaround Time (MTT). This depends on the aircraft type and the airline.
It defines the minimum time an aircraft must remain at the stand. However, these times
also represent best-case scenarios and are calculated very optimistically.
I:Are these MTT values stored in the system in a standardized form?
E:Yes, the values are stored in our system as master data for each aircraft type and
airline. They are supplied by the airlines, and from an operational perspective, we can
only assess how realistic they are. An overseas long-haul flight, for example, requires
considerably more extensive catering, which makes the processes take longer.
Another factor is passengers with reduced mobility (PRM), who are present on most
flights. They usually board first. If only one or two people pass through the door at
the beginning, the process has formally started, even though the nominal boarding rate
per minute is very low at that point. It is therefore a question of definition whether the
start is tied to the first passenger scan at the gate or to a continuous flow of passengers.
A-CDM Applications and Operational Disruptions
I:There are publicly available applications in the App Store, such as the A-CDM App.
Are these suitable as data sources?
E:You can view a lot of information on individual flights there. You would need to
check whether structured bulk data can be exported.
I:How frequently do unforeseen disruptions occur in practice, such as full overhead bins,
unavailable fuel trucks, or technical checks?
E:A turnaround in which absolutely everything goes according to plan is rather excep-
tional. Typical disruptions include carry-on baggage having to be loaded into the hold;
passengers failing to show up and their baggage having to be unloaded; maintenance staff
having to perform checks at short notice; additional catering supplies being required; or
staffing shortages in ground handling.
52

Nevertheless, not every irregularity immediately causes a flight delay. Often, the Target
Off-Block Time (TOBT) is adjusted in the system. If an aircraft requires more time,
the TOBT is moved to a later time. This is a standard procedure.
Time Buffers and Seasonal Flight Schedules
I:Do airlines routinely include time buffers in the MTT?
E:As far as I know, the MTT itself contains no buffers; it represents only the theoretical
minimum. Buffers arise from the seasonal flight schedule. Many aircraft have longer
ground times than the MTT requires; some remain on the ground for several hours or
overnight. With a ground time of six or seven hours, delays in the inbound process have
little operational impact on the outbound flight.
Capacity Limits and Resource Conflicts in Ground Handling
I:How many turnarounds can take place simultaneously? Where are the bottlenecks?
E:We have approximately 80 stands. How many of these can be served simultaneously
by the ground handlers depends on the contracts. The teams work sequentially: a
team places the chocks, connects the ground power unit, secures the passenger boarding
bridge, and then moves on to the next stand.
A frequent bottleneck is the availability of loading crew members and load supervisors.
According to the prescribed procedure, the team must be ready at the stand before the
aircraft arrives so that the chocks can be placed immediately. Often, the crew is not yet
there. In such cases, the marshallers or follow-me drivers often place the chocks so that
the aircraft can be reported as on-block before the ground handling crew arrives.
Simulation Requirements, Predictions, and Data Quality
I:What would an ideal simulation need to achieve from an airport operations perspec-
tive?
E:In an ideal world, it would represent all exceptional disruptive events, such as baggage
offloading, medical incidents, contamination, or technical defects. Realistically, however,
the most important task would be to model the core processes reliably and calculate a
dependable Predicted Ready Time (PRDT): when will the aircraft actually be ready for
departure?
Current predictions at the airport often become accurate only 10 to 15 minutes before
off-block. For operational planning, a reliable prediction one to two hours in advance,
or directly at on-block, would be extremely valuable.
I:Are publicly accessible A-CDM data sufficient for this?
53

E:A-CDM provides useful milestones such as TSAT or TOBT. However, the level of
detail is insufficient for the internal turnaround processes. Reports such as Ground
Handling Completed or Actual Ready Time (ARDT) are partly based on manual entries
by staff at the stand and are not always accurate. This is often referred to as the
“turnaround black box. ” For this reason, computer vision systems are being adopted in
many places to capture timestamps for individual activities automatically.

modeling?
E:We are currently evaluating computer vision systems. I will ask and get back to you.
I:Thank you very much; that would be a great help.
