Abstract
This thesis investigates data-informed object-centric simulation of airport
turnaround using a prepared Berlin Brandenburg aircraft-interval cohort and an
existing OCPN airport model. Available observations inform demand and candi-
date aircraft context, while literature assumptions describe unobserved services.
Object-centric logs inspected in Promenade expose shared-resource queues, paral-
lel completion constraints and case-extraction pitfalls. A controlled pilot runs six
scenarios on each of two dates with five replications per cell. All sixty runs com-
plete, yielding 15,270 simulated executions based on 509 reused empirical interval
cases. With inherited curfew disabled, literature timing assumptions reduce mean
simulated block duration by 7.53 and 7.04 minutes compared with original tim-
ing functions. Increasing fueling capacity greatly reduces local waiting but changes
overall turnaround by only 0.62 and 0.22 minutes. Inherited policy materially am-
plifies apparent timing effects and contains a confirmed UTC/local-time mismatch.
Findings support conditional sensitivity analysis rather than validated BER service-
time prediction or optimal staffing. Long stays, incomplete traffic, assumed passen-
ger demand and missing ground-service observations remain limitations. This draft
documents completed work and explicitly identifies unfinished empirical evaluation.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Aircraft turnaround is the period between an aircraft arriving at its parking
position and leaving for its next flight. Ground teams unload baggage, clean and
refuel the aircraft, and prepare it for boarding. Some activities proceed in parallel,
whereas others require preceding work to finish. Flights also compete for shared
stands, cleaning teams, fueling vehicles and pushback tugs. A delay in one service
can therefore affect both its own turnaround and the resources available to other
aircraft.
This thesis studies those interactions using an object-centric simulation informed
by available Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER) observations. It distinguishes ob-
served input data, transferred literature assumptions and generated simulation
events. The implemented study links finite arrival demand to persistent aircraft
identities, exports object-centric event logs and compares service-time, policy and
capacity scenarios. The results reported here describe the completed simulation pilot
as of 13 November 2026; they do not constitute a validated digital twin of BER.
1.1 Background
Airport Collaborative Decision Making (A-CDM) supports the exchange of
timely operational information between airport partners and the Network Manager,
particularly around turnaround and pre-departure processes [1]. However, access
to an A-CDM-related application does not imply access to every internal ground-
service event. A boundary interval can contain several concurrent activities, waiting

The student’s contribution is the empirical demand adapter, traceable pairing and
assumption management, literature-informed timing changes, event-log analysis and
controlled comparison of scenarios.
1.2 Motivation
The motivation is to explain simulated waiting and readiness through interact-
ing activities and resources. Adding capacity may remove a local queue without
substantially changing the aircraft’s final completion time. Conversely, a policy rule
can create a long apparent service delay without a resource shortage. Distinguishing
these mechanisms is necessary before drawing conclusions from a turnaround-time
distribution.
The study also examines how much can be learned from incomplete operational
observations. Available data support arrival demand and candidate aircraft char-
acteristics, but do not identify all internal service durations. A useful model must
expose this distinction rather than treat generated service events as additional em-
pirical evidence.
1.3 Problem statement
The original research objective is to use A-CDM information to calibrate an
object-centric turnaround simulation and investigate resource allocation. In the
available collection, internal service start/end times and measured block times are
absent. The implemented study therefore combines OpenSky boundary proxies with

1.4 Research questions
The original research questions are retained:
RQ1:How can A-CDM Data be used to calibrate activity durations and temporal
dependencies in an OCPN Simulation?
RQ2:How do dependencies between aircraft, stands, and shared ground-service
resources contribute to waiting times and delay propagation at terminal stands
and remote stands in an OCPN simulation?
RQ3:How can the calibrated simulation be used to evaluate resource-allocation
strategies and identify configurations that reduce turnaround delays and re-

RQ1 is addressed by an observability audit and evidence-informed model in-
puts, with clear limits on activity-level calibration. RQ2 is investigated through
shared-resource waiting, parallel-branch readiness and object-centric extraction. A
controlled terminal-versus-remote stand comparison and a quantified inter-flight
propagation analysis remain open. RQ3 is addressed by repeated conditional capac-
ity experiments, without claiming optimal real BER staffing. Predictive accuracy
evaluation is a methodological requirement across these questions, rather than a
replacement definition of RQ2.

Related work and methodological

2.1 Turnaround dependencies and service timing
Schultz et al. describe stochastic turnaround processes and the role of the critical
path in disturbance propagation [4]. This supports representing concurrent ground
services and their synchronization explicitly. It does not imply that a duration dis-
tribution fitted elsewhere transfers unchanged to BER.
This study transfers reference assumptions for narrowbody cleaning, fueling and
boarding from that work. Their implementation is detailed in Chapter 3. These are
literature-informed scenario inputs, not estimates recovered from the Berlin bound-
ary intervals. No universal low-cost versus full-service multiplier is inferred from
those observations.
Schultz’s later study develops and evaluates a calibrated stochastic passenger-
boarding model [5]. It motivates treating boarding as an important and variable
turnaround component. The present implementation uses a passenger-dependent
mean reference; it does not reproduce that detailed stochastic boarding model or its
empirical validation.
2.2 A-CDM and available observations
EUROCONTROL describes A-CDM as a collaboration and information-sharing
approach involving airport operators, aircraft operators, ground handlers, air traf-

Software citations document the tools used and are distinct from empirical evidence

2.4 Position of the present work
The contribution lies in connecting a traceable empirical demand cohort to an
existing object-centric model and making the resulting analyses auditable. It com-
bines evidence separation, case-definition diagnostics, repeated timing/policy com-
parisons and resource sensitivity. It does not claim a replication of a particular LAX
study, a JFK evaluation, a trained machine-learning duration predictor or empirical
conformance checking: these have not been completed in the reported work.

3.1 Data provenance and cohort construction
The prepared OpenSky movement table contains 22,635 records: 11,316 arrivals
and 11,319 departures. Candidate BER ground intervals link an observed arrival to
the following observed BER departure of the same ICAO24 identifier. The prepared
core cohort contains 10,940 interval rows: 8,951 in the 15-minute to six-hour band
and 1,989 in the six-to-24-hour band. Each row represents a candidate aircraft-linked
interval, not a certified aircraft rotation.
The intended collection begins on 1 October 2026. The core file also includes 54
arrivals on 30 September paired to departures entering the requested period. Arrival
observations in the file extend through 9 November. Accordingly, this version does
not claim complete data coverage through 13 November. Temporal coverage, API
availability and filtering must be distinguished from the number of interval rows
retained.

3.2 Daily experimental inputs
The daily models use 271 cases and 235 distinct aircraft on 1 November, and
238 cases and 212 aircraft on 8 November. Supported candidate aircraft types exist
for 216 and 180 input rows, respectively. This row-level coverage does not mean
every final aircraft configuration is observed: the first case establishes a persistent

configuration. Later conflicting candidates have not been reconciled.
The two cohorts include 39 and 44 intervals longer than six hours. These long
stays are not automatically equivalent to active ground handling. Matched-core de-
mand also excludes unmatched and other discarded movements, so the simulated
resource competition is conditional on this cohort rather than a complete represen-
tation of airport traffic.
3.3 Baseline attribution and study modifications
The supervisor’s original extended model is preserved unchanged. Its six par-
allel handling threads, resource interaction logic, vehicle positions, fuel supply
mechanisms and process dependencies are inherited contributions. A same-demand
original-timing comparator retains the original service functions within the adapted
demand model; it is not the untouched example.
The study replaces indefinite synthetic generation with finite timed demand.
Inbound availability is released using the observed arrival boundary proxy. A per-
sistent aircraft identity is required for the paired rotation and returned after simu-
lated takeoff. Subsequent cases cannot use the same aircraft while it remains in the
model. Synthetic outstation travel and the inherited inbound curfew/extra approach
delay are bypassed; landing roll, runway contention and taxi-in remain simulated.
Observed outbound times are retained outside the model as evaluation references
and do not force simulated departures.

The inherited 25-stand geometry, runway dimensions, resource capacities, taxi

Models begin each daily run with free stands and resources, without calibrated
overnight occupancy or a warm-up history. These choices restrict interpretation of
queues and resource experiments.

3.6 Controlled experiment
Six scenarios are run on each date, with five independent replications per cell:
60 runs across 12 date/scenario cells. Four scenarios cross literature/original timing
with inherited/disabled curfew. Two further scenarios use literature timings and dis-
abled curfew, reducing cleaning teams from nine to six or increasing fuel units from
four hydrants plus four tankers to six plus six. Demand and remaining assumptions
are held fixed within each date.
The design was fixed before these runs, informed by the earlier pilot. It was not
independently preregistered. The installed WebAssembly API does not expose seed
control; replications use fresh runtime randomness and comparisons are unpaired.
All 15,270 turnaround executions reuse 509 empirical cases across scenarios and
replications. They are not 15,270 independent observed flights.
For a metricm, the statistical unit is the replication-level mean¯mr. Scenario
means and Student-t95% intervals are computed over five such means. Differences
use independent-run Welch 95% intervals. No multiplicity correction is applied; com-
parisons are exploratory. These intervals quantify simulation sampling variation con-
ditional on fixed inputs, not uncertainty in load factors, capacities, transferred dis-
tributions or model structure.

3.7 Metrics and verification
Simulated block turnaround is pushback start/off-block minus in-block. Fuel
waiting is handling start to fuel dispatch, excluding subsequent travel; cleaning
waiting is deplaning completion to cleaning start; tug waiting is aircraft-ready to
tug allocation. These definitions are retained when comparing scenarios.
For each turnaround, the six handling branches are compared using output-token
availability times at the synchronization join, including post-transition arc delays.
The last-ready branch identifies a synchronization constraint in that realization;
frequency alone is not a causal bottleneck test. Resource-count interventions provide
an additional sensitivity check.
Technical verification requires all expected cases to depart, the timed-demand
queue to be empty, aircraft to return to their availability pool, relevant resources to
return and metrics to be finite/nonnegative. All 60 runs pass these checks, including
model-hashandunique-casecoverageaudits.Technicalcompletiondoesnotestablish
predictive accuracy.

4.3 Case-definition diagnostic
The 1 November Promenade overview has two connected object components,
with 94% of objects in the largest. Reusable resources connect many rotations; a
connected-component case definition would therefore merge most of the day. A lead-
ing Turnaround extraction returns 271 executions and 271 variants at the selected
settings, but expanded executions can include shared-resource events. One expanded
execution displays approximately 16.7 hours, which is not that turnaround’s paired
block duration. The analysis therefore uses direct paired milestones for duration
statistics. A SQL-discovered object-centric directly-follows graph was produced; the
separate metro-map layout was canceled after stalling and is not a completed result.
4.4 Observed proxy discrepancies
The first 1 November literature run has a simulated planned-arrival-to-takeoff
proxy median of 79.63 minutes, close to the observed boundary-proxy median of
79.12. However, their means are 85.63 versus 156.17 minutes, with mean bias−70.55
minutes and mean absolute discrepancy 92.09 minutes. On 8 November, mean bias
is−101.60minutes and mean absolute discrepancy 116.00 minutes.
A close median therefore conceals a poor distributional match. The current sim-
ulator starts handling after arrival and departs when ready, while observations can
include parking and schedule buffers. Service durations should not be inflated merely
to absorb that mismatch. Pairing review, stay stratification and independently jus-
tified departure scheduling are required before accuracy claims.

Discussion and threats to validity
5.1 Answers supported by the current evidence
5.1.1 RQ1: data-informed calibration
The available observations support finite arrival demand, candidate aircraft pair-
ing and selected contextual checks. They do not identify cleaning, fueling or board-
ing durations: multiple services and waiting can occur within one observed bound-
ary interval. Transferred distributions and explicit assumptions make an executable
model possible, but must not be described as fitted BER service-time estimates.
Additional service start/end observations, passenger counts and reliable block times-
tamps would strengthen activity-level calibration.
5.1.2 RQ2: dependencies, queues and propagation
Object-linked logs show how shared resource availability and parallel branches
contribute to simulated waiting and readiness. Fuel dispatch has the clearest local
queue in the initial runs, yet cabin/boarding is usually the final handling branch.
The capacity intervention supports the interpretation that removing fuel waiting
can have limited effect on final completion because work overlaps.
This is a partial answer. Persistent aircraft and reusable resources permit con-
tention in the model, but the current experiments do not quantify how an exogenous
delay propagates across flights. Nor do they provide a controlled terminal-versus-
remote comparison: stand context and bus/access assumptions are not empirically

5.1.3 RQ3: conditional resource evaluation
Repeated capacity comparisons demonstrate how to evaluate local queues along-
side overall turnaround and uncertainty. The findings favor reporting both levels
rather than selecting a configuration using queue length alone. However, only two
capacity alternatives were tested, with assumed resources and no cost objective. The
current evidence cannot establish an optimal resource configuration or real staffing
recommendation.
5.2 Policy and clock semantics
BER’s official guidance describes a core night-flight restriction from 00:00 to
05:00 and constraints in the adjacent 23:00–24:00 and 05:00–06:00 windows [9].
The inherited model instead applies its helper before pre-boarding and pushback
clearance. Those applications do not constitute a complete operational movement-
eligibility model.
The installed CPNsim runtime was probed at epoch2026-11-01T00:00:00Z: its
hour helper returns zero, while Berlin local hour is one. Engine source also uses UTC
[8]. Thus the inherited 00:00–05:00 condition corresponds to 01:00–06:00 local time
on both study dates. This mismatch is confirmed, rather than a possible explanation
inferred solely from plotted tails.
The controlled experiment deliberately retains the inherited implementation in
its policy cells and disables the entire helper in the diagnostic cells. Neither is pre-
sented as a verified BER replacement. A subsequent version must use Europe/Berlin
time, including daylight-saving changes across the full collection, and distinguish
movement restrictions, shoulder windows, exceptions and independent cabin work.
The present analysis does not make a legal-compliance determination.

5.4 Statistical and analytical validity
Five replications per cell provide pilot uncertainty conditional on a fixed model.
They do not propagate input or structural uncertainty. Runs are unpaired, random
seeds are unavailable, and exact stochastic reruns cannot be promised. Multiple
exploratory contrasts are reported without correction. Intervals crossing zero do not
prove no effect, and a barely excluding interval is not strong operational evidence.
The timing comparison changes three services together; it cannot assign the
result to cleaning, fueling or boarding separately, or distinguish changes in mean
from variability. November 8 is a later-date scenario with no outcome-based tuning
in this pilot, but is not a completed held-out predictive validation. Formal empirical
conformance, contact/remote stratification, resource time utilization, cost analysis
and external replication remain incomplete.
Promenade extraction scope is another source of analytical error. Shared re-
sources connect rotations, and expanded executions can contain events outside the
focal block interval. The chosen one-to-one milestone pairs and explicit metric defi-
nitions avoid treating such expanded durations as aircraft turnaround observations.
5.5 Remaining work before stronger conclusions
The next model version should correct local-time movement policy, resolve
aircraft-type candidates and distinguish operational turnarounds from long stays.
Evaluation should then use independently selected dates and matching observable

Chapter 6
Conclusion
The completed study connects a prepared BER aircraft-interval cohort to an
existing object-centric airport simulation, exports interpretable logs and evaluates
controlled timing, policy and resource scenarios. Attribution separates the supervi-
sor’s original process model from the student’s empirical adapter, timing changes,
provenance controls and analyses.
The 60-replication pilot completes 15,270 simulated turnaround executions based
on 509 reused observed interval candidates. Literature timing assumptions shorten
mean simulated block duration by approximately seven minutes when the inherited
curfew helper is disabled. Much larger differences under inherited policy expose
sensitivity to policy logic. Increasing fueling capacity removes most dispatch waiting
but produces a much smaller overall turnaround change, consistent with overlap
among parallel handling branches.
These findings provide partial answers to RQ1–RQ3. Available data inform de-
mand and selected context, while unobserved services remain assumptions. Object-
centric analysis exposes dependencies and extraction pitfalls, while stand-specific
and inter-flight propagation comparisons remain open. Repeated capacity scenarios
support conditional evaluation, not an optimal actual BER staffing plan.
The main practical requirement is to preserve the distinction between observa-
tion, assumption and generated behavior. Further work must correct the local-time
movement policy, represent long stays and evaluate matching observable quantities
on separate dates. This draft records the completed pilot and its limitations; it does
not claim that the full empirical validation or thesis project is finished.
