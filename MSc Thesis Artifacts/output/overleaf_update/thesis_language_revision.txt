Abstract
Detailed records of airport ground services are often unavailable, although
turnaround performance depends on their timing and shared resources. This the-
sis investigates how limited Berlin Brandenburg Airport observations can sup-
port an object-centric turnaround simulation. We adapt an existing OCPN airport
model, prepare aircraft-linked demand and export generated events for analysis in
Promenade. Observed inputs are kept separate from literature-based service timings
and other assumptions. The analysis examines resource waiting, parallel completion
and the interpretation of object-centric executions. A controlled pilot compares six
scenarios on two dates, with five independent runs per scenario and date. All 60 runs
complete, producing 15,270 simulated executions from 509 reused empirical interval
cases. With inherited curfew disabled, literature timing assumptions reduce mean
simulated block duration by 7.53 and 7.04 minutes compared with the original ser-
vice functions. Increasing fuel capacity substantially reduces dispatch waiting, while
its effect on final turnaround is much smaller. The results also reveal an interaction
between service timing and inherited policy, including a confirmed UTC/local-time
mismatch.Thesefindingssupportconditionalanalysisoftheadaptedmodel.Missing
internal service observations, long stays and assumed operational context limit con-
clusions about actual BER performance. Further empirical evaluation is required
before claiming validated calibration or optimal resource allocation.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Aircraft turnaround is the period between an aircraft’s arrival at a parking posi-
tion and its departure for the next flight. During this period, several teams prepare
the aircraft by unloading baggage, cleaning the cabin, refueling and boarding pas-
sengers. These activities follow different dependencies. Some can take place at the
same time, while others can start only after preceding work has finished. Aircraft
also share stands, service vehicles and ground teams. As a result, the availability of
one resource can affect several turnarounds.
Understanding these interactions requires information about both the activi-
ties and the resources involved. However, the available operational records do not
describe every ground service. They mainly provide information about flight move-
ments and selected departure milestones. This creates a gap between the process we
want to study and the behavior we can directly observe.
This thesis investigates how an object-centric simulation can support turnaround
analysis under these conditions. We adapt an existing airport model using prepared
observations from Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER), document the assumptions
used for missing information and analyze the generated event logs. We then compare
service-time assumptions, policy rules and resource capacities through repeated sim-
ulation. The reported results concern the completed pilot study. Further empirical
evaluation is required before the model can support conclusions about actual BER
performance.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background
Airport Collaborative Decision Making (A-CDM) supports information exchange
among airport partners and the Network Manager, with particular attention to
turnaround and pre-departure processes [1]. This information helps partners coordi-
nate their activities. Nevertheless, an A-CDM-related application does not necessar-
ily provide the start and end times of every ground service. A time interval between
two milestones may contain several activities, waiting and parking. Its total duration
is therefore insufficient to determine how long each internal activity took.
Object-centric event logs provide a way to retain the relationships between these
activities and the objects involved. An event can be linked to a turnaround, an air-
craft and a service resource. OCEL 2.0 supports these relationships, their qualifiers
and changes in object attributes [2]. This is useful when a cleaning team or a fueling
vehicle serves several aircraft during the same day.
The simulation used in this thesis is based on theAirport Ground Handling
(Extended)model provided by the supervisor, István Koren, in OCPN Studio [3].
Its original process structure and resource logic are retained as attributed prior work.
Our modifications concern the empirical demand, aircraft pairing, selected activity
timings, analysis procedures and controlled experiments.
1.2 Motivation
A departure time alone does not explain why an aircraft was ready late. The
aircraft may have been waiting for a resource, receiving a service or waiting for
another parallel activity to finish. These explanations require different operational
responses. For example, increasing the number of fueling units can reduce the fuel
queue without substantially shortening turnaround if boarding is still in progress.
Simulation allows us to examine these relationships under defined conditions. It
also allows assumptions to be changed individually while keeping other inputs fixed.
However, the resulting events remain synthetic. Their detail must not be mistaken
for additional observations of real ground handling. The motivation of this study is
therefore twofold: to investigate dependencies within the model and to make clear
which conclusions the available evidence supports.

1. Introduction
1.3 Problem Statement
The research objective is to use A-CDM information to support the calibration
of an object-centric turnaround simulation and the evaluation of resource alloca-
tion. The collected data do not contain reliable internal service start/end times or
measured block times. The implemented study therefore combines OpenSky flight-
boundary observations with A-CDM reference records. Literature and explicitly
stated assumptions supply the missing service inputs.
Theproblemistoconstructanexecutableandtraceablemodelfromthesesources
without treating assumed durations as measured values. A second challenge is to
interpret its outputs correctly. Changes in simulated turnaround may result from
service timing, resource availability or policy logic. These effects must be examined
separately before drawing conclusions about calibration or allocation.
1.4 Research Questions
The following research questions guide the study:
RQ1:How can A-CDM Data be used to calibrate activity durations and temporal
dependencies in an OCPN Simulation?
RQ2:How do dependencies between aircraft, stands, and shared ground-service
resources contribute to waiting times and delay propagation at terminal stands
and remote stands in an OCPN simulation?
RQ3:How can the calibrated simulation be used to evaluate resource-allocation
strategies and identify configurations that reduce turnaround delays and re-
source contention?
The current work addresses these questions to different extents. For RQ1, we
examine which model inputs can be supported by the collected data. For RQ2,
we analyze resource waiting and the synchronization of parallel activities. A con-
trolled comparison of terminal and remote stands, and a quantified analysis of delay
propagation between flights, remain open. For RQ3, we evaluate selected capacity
changes under fixed assumptions. Predictive accuracy is relevant to the evaluation
of the model as a whole; it does not replace the dependency-focused meaning of
RQ2.

Chapter 2
Related Work and Methodological
Background
This chapter introduces the work used to guide the study. We first consider
turnaround timing and the available operational observations. We then discuss
object-centric simulation and a closely related trace-reconstruction thesis. The fi-
nal section explains the position of the present work.
2.1 Turnaround Dependencies and Activity Timing
Schultz et al. describe stochastic turnaround processes and discuss how distur-
bances on a critical path can affect later operations [4]. Their work supports a model
in which concurrent services must synchronize before the aircraft can proceed. It
also provides reference assumptions used here for narrowbody cleaning, fueling and
boarding.
Transferring a reference distribution does not establish that it describes BER
operations. In this study, the distributions are used as stated model inputs. We do
not estimate them from the Berlin boundary intervals or derive a universal timing
difference between low-cost and full-service airlines.
Schultz later presents a calibrated stochastic boarding model and evaluates
boarding scenarios [5]. This work shows the importance of considering passenger
boarding in turnaround analysis. Our implementation uses a passenger-dependent
mean reference, rather than reproducing that detailed stochastic model or its vali-
dation.

2. Related Work and Methodological Background
2.2 Operational Observations and the Data Gap
EUROCONTROL describes A-CDM as an approach for sharing operational in-
formation among airport operators, aircraft operators, ground handlers, air traffic
control and the Network Manager [1]. This provides the operational context for mile-
stone analysis. Whether a particular collection contains enough detail for activity-
level calibration must still be checked separately.
The OpenSky REST API provides aircraft identifiers, first and last observation
timestamps, and estimated airports for flight records [6]. These fields support candi-
date aircraft pairing. However, they do not directly measure arrival at or departure
from a stand. Accordingly, the observed boundary interval and the simulated block
duration are treated as different quantities throughout this thesis.
2.3 Object-Centric Simulation and Event Logs
OCEL 2.0 represents events, objects, attributes and their relationships [2]. We
use this representation to retain the links between generated turnaround events and
reusable resources. Promenade provides exploratory views of these logs and their
temporal behavior [7]. These views help explain the simulation output, but cannot
independently confirm that its input assumptions are realistic.
OCPN Studio provides the modeling environment and the extended airport ex-
ample [3]. The official CPNsim runtime executes colored Petri nets [8]. Our runs use
its installed WebAssembly version 0.6.2. These software references document the
implementation environment; they are not empirical evidence about airport opera-
tions.
2.4 Turnaround Trace Reconstruction from Sparse
Data
Tkacenko’s bachelor thesis investigates the generation of detailed turnaround
traces when only limited observations are available [9]. The study models narrow-
body operations at Frankfurt Airport incpn-py, combines domain knowledge with
duration assumptions and exports simulated traces as OCEL 2.0. Candidate traces
are filtered using observed in-block and off-block boundaries. The evaluation dis-

tinguishes model completion properties, empirical activity-duration agreement and
relational checks on the exported data.
This work is relevant because it makes a distinction that also matters here:
agreement with a total turnaround duration does not establish that the internal
activity durations are correct [9, Sections 6.2 and 7.4]. The accepted traces in that
study show different levels of agreement across activities. This supports the need to
report the limits of reconstruction from sparse observations.
The present study follows a different evaluation procedure. We release demand
using OpenSky observation boundaries and retain A-CDM records as references. We
do not filter simulation runs to make them fit observed off-block times. Instead, we
compare timing, policy and capacity assumptions under the same daily demand.
The resulting differences describe the behavior of the adapted model. They do not
establish that the missing Berlin service events have been accurately reconstructed.
The uncertainty measures also serve different purposes. Tkacenko compares indi-
vidual ground-truth activity durations with central empirical intervals of simulated
durations. Our controlled experiment calculates confidence intervals for differences
between replication-level means. An interval describing activity variation is therefore
not interchangeable with an interval describing uncertainty in a mean comparison.
2.5 Position of the Present Study
The contribution of this work is the connection of a traceable Berlin demand
cohort to the supplied extended model, followed by object-centric analysis and re-
peated scenario comparisons. We attribute the original airport process model to
the supervisor and cite Tkacenko’s thesis as related methodological work. The two
implementations are not assumed to be identical.
The completed study does not include a JFK evaluation, a replication of a par-
ticular LAX study, a trained machine-learning duration predictor or empirical con-
formance checking. These remain outside the results reported here.

Chapter 3
Data and Simulation Methodology
This chapter describes how the available observations are converted into simula-
tion inputs. We first define the data sources and the daily cohorts. We then explain
the changes to the supplied model, the service-time assumptions and the OCEL
export. Finally, we present the controlled experiment, its metrics and the technical
checks. The procedure keeps observed values, literature inputs and generated events
separate.
3.1 Data provenance and cohort construction
The prepared OpenSky movement table contains 22,635 records: 11,316 arrivals
and 11,319 departures. Candidate BER ground intervals link an observed arrival to
the following observed BER departure of the same ICAO24 identifier. The prepared
core cohort contains 10,940 interval rows: 8,951 in the 15-minute to six-hour band
and 1,989 in the six-to-24-hour band. Each row represents a candidate aircraft-linked
interval, not a certified aircraft rotation.
The intended collection begins on 1 October 2026. The core file also includes 54
arrivals on 30 September paired to departures entering the requested period. Arrival
observations in the file extend through 9 November. Accordingly, this version does
not claim complete data coverage through 13 November. Temporal coverage, API
availability and filtering must be distinguished from the number of interval rows
retained.

the inbound flight and the first observation of the outbound flight. For casei, this

3.2 Daily experimental inputs
The daily models use 271 cases and 235 distinct aircraft on 1 November, and
238 cases and 212 aircraft on 8 November. Supported candidate aircraft types exist
for 216 and 180 input rows, respectively. This row-level coverage does not mean
every final aircraft configuration is observed: the first case establishes a persistent
model configuration, and missing or unsupported types fall back to an assumed A320
configuration. Later conflicting candidates have not been reconciled.
The two cohorts include 39 and 44 intervals longer than six hours. A long in-
terval may include parking or a timetable buffer rather than continuous ground
handling. In addition, movements without an accepted pair are excluded. The simu-
lated queues therefore describe competition within the selected cohort. They cannot
be interpreted as queues caused by all BER traffic.

3.6 Controlled experiment
Six scenarios are run on each date, with five independent replications per cell:
60 runs across 12 date/scenario cells. Four scenarios cross literature/original timing
with inherited/disabled curfew. Two further scenarios use literature timings and dis-
abled curfew, reducing cleaning teams from nine to six or increasing fuel units from
four hydrants plus four tankers to six plus six. Demand and remaining assumptions
are held fixed within each date.

3. Data and Simulation Methodology
The earlier exploratory analysis informed this design. We fixed the scenario set-
tings before executing the new runs, but the study was not independently prereg-
istered. The installed WebAssembly API does not expose seed control; replications
use fresh runtime randomness and comparisons are unpaired. All 15,270 turnaround
executions reuse 509 empirical cases across scenarios and replications. They are not
15,270 independent observed flights.
For each metricm, we first calculate a mean within each simulation run, de-
noted by¯mr. We then use the five run means to calculate the scenario mean and its
Student-t95% confidence interval. Thus, the statistical unit is a simulation repli-
cation rather than an individual flight. Differences use independent-run Welch 95%
intervals. No multiplicity correction is applied; comparisons are exploratory. These
intervals quantify simulation sampling variation conditional on fixed inputs, not
uncertainty in load factors, capacities, transferred distributions or model structure.
3.7 Metrics and verification
Simulated block turnaround is pushback start/off-block minus in-block. Fuel
waiting is handling start to fuel dispatch, excluding subsequent travel; cleaning
waiting is deplaning completion to cleaning start; tug waiting is aircraft-ready to
tug allocation. These definitions are retained when comparing scenarios.
For each turnaround, we record when the output of each of the six handling
branches becomes available at the join. These times include delays on the arcs after
transitions. The branch arriving last determines when this part of the process can
synchronizeinthatrun.Ahighlast-readyfrequencyisdescriptiveevidence,however;
itdoesnotaloneshowhowmuchtheoverallturnaroundwouldimproveifthatbranch
changed. We therefore also test changes in resource counts.
Technical verification requires all expected cases to depart, the timed-demand
queue to be empty, aircraft to return to their availability pool, relevant resources to
return and metrics to be finite/nonnegative. All 60 runs pass these checks, including
model-hashandunique-casecoverageaudits.Technicalcompletiondoesnotestablish
predictive accuracy.

4.2 Resource queues and handling dependencies
In the individual literature-timing runs, mean fuel-dispatch waiting is 3.785 min-
utes on 1 November and 1.255 minutes on 8 November. Cleaning-team waiting is
0.018 and approximately zero minutes; tug-allocation waiting is 0.033 and approx-
imately zero. The 1 November 90th-percentile fuel waiting time is 14.20 minutes,
with a maximum of 38.28 minutes.
Nevertheless, the cabin/boarding branch is last ready in 230 of 271 turnarounds
(84.9%) and 192 of 238 (80.7%). Fuel is last in only 21 and 10 cases; baggage han-
dling is last in 20 and 36. This result distinguishes local waiting from final readiness.
An aircraft can wait for fuel while cabin work continues. Reducing that wait may
therefore leave the final completion time almost unchanged. The branch counts de-
scribe what happened at the join in these runs; a controlled change is needed to
assess its effect on overall duration.
These observations partially address RQ2 by exposing parallel dependencies and
resource contention within the simulation. They do not establish real terminal-
versus-remote stand differences or quantify empirical inter-flight delay propagation.
Workload counts per shared resource must also not be called time utilization without
occupancy-duration calculations.

block duration. The analysis therefore uses direct paired milestones for duration
statistics. A SQL-discovered object-centric directly-follows graph was produced; the

Chapter 5
Discussion and Threats to Validity
This chapter explains what the results contribute to the research questions. We
then discuss the inherited policy and the main limitations of the data, model and
analysis. The aim is to distinguish findings supported by the experiment from con-
clusions that still require further observations or experiments.
5.1 Answers supported by the current evidence
5.1.1 RQ1: data-informed calibration
The collected observations support a finite arrival schedule, candidate aircraft
pairings and checks on selected flight attributes. They do not provide enough in-
formation to estimate cleaning, fueling or boarding durations separately. Several
activities and waiting periods can occur within the same boundary interval. We can
therefore use the data to construct demand, but the internal services still require
literature inputs and assumptions. Service start/end records, passenger counts and
reliable block timestamps would be needed for stronger activity-level calibration.
5.1.2 RQ2: dependencies, queues and propagation
Object-linked logs show how shared resource availability and parallel branches
contribute to simulated waiting and readiness. Fuel dispatch has the clearest local
queue in the initial runs, yet cabin/boarding is usually the final handling branch.
The capacity intervention supports the interpretation that removing fuel waiting
can have limited effect on final completion because work overlaps.
24

5. Discussion and Threats to Validity
The answer to RQ2 remains partial. Aircraft identities and reusable resources
allow contention across turnarounds, but we have not measured how an imposed
delay passes from one flight to another. We also have not isolated differences between
terminal and remote stands. Such a comparison requires reliable stand classifications
and explicit assumptions for bus and access time. It must also control the demand
and initial resource state so that the source of a difference can be explained.
5.1.3 RQ3: conditional resource evaluation
Repeated capacity comparisons demonstrate how to evaluate local queues along-
side overall turnaround and uncertainty. The findings favor reporting both levels
rather than selecting a configuration using queue length alone. However, only two
capacity alternatives were tested, with assumed resources and no cost objective. The
current evidence cannot establish an optimal resource configuration or real staffing
recommendation.
5.2 Policy and clock semantics
BER’s official guidance describes a core night-flight restriction from 00:00 to
05:00 and constraints in the adjacent 23:00–24:00 and 05:00–06:00 windows [10].
The inherited model instead applies its helper before pre-boarding and pushback
clearance. Those applications do not constitute a complete operational movement-
eligibility model.
The installed CPNsim runtime was probed at epoch2026-11-01T00:00:00Z: its
hour helper returns zero, while Berlin local hour is one. Engine source also uses UTC
[8]. Thus the inherited 00:00–05:00 condition corresponds to 01:00–06:00 local time
on both study dates. This mismatch is confirmed, rather than a possible explanation
inferred solely from plotted tails.
The controlled experiment deliberately retains the inherited implementation in
its policy cells and disables the entire helper in the diagnostic cells. Neither is pre-
sented as a verified BER replacement. A subsequent version must use Europe/Berlin
time, including daylight-saving changes across the full collection, and distinguish
movement restrictions, shoulder windows, exceptions and independent cabin work.
The present analysis does not make a legal-compliance determination.

5. Discussion and Threats to Validity
5.3 Data and construct validity
ICAO24 adjacency provides candidate rotations rather than certified ground
movements. Missing flights, airport-estimation errors or identifier issues may af-
fect pairing. OpenSky observation boundaries and simulated block milestones are
different constructs. A-CDM reference matches and a timezone hypothesis do not
remove these differences. The observed-proxy discrepancies should therefore be re-
ported without calling them block-time prediction errors.
Long stays, planned departure buffers and overnight parking are not modeled.
The selected cohort also omits competing traffic, and every daily run begins with free
resources. These choices can materially alter queues. Fictitious geometry, passenger
load, PRM demand, carrier class, optional services and non-narrowbody functions
further restrict transfer to real BER operations.
5.4 Statistical and analytical validity
The confidence intervals describe variation across five replications under fixed
model assumptions. They do not include uncertainty in passenger loads, capacities
or the process structure. The runs are unpaired because the runtime does not expose
seedcontrol.Repeatingtheprocedurewillthereforegeneratenewstochasticoutputs.
We also report several exploratory comparisons without a multiple-comparison ad-
justment. An interval containing zero is not proof of no effect, while an interval that
only narrowly excludes zero should be interpreted cautiously.
The timing comparison changes cleaning, fueling and boarding together. It there-
fore cannot determine which individual service explains the difference. It also cannot
separate the effect of a changed mean duration from that of changed variability.
November 8 is a later-date scenario with no outcome-based tuning in this pilot, but
is not a completed held-out predictive validation. Formal empirical conformance,
contact/remote stratification, resource time utilization, cost analysis and external
replication remain incomplete.
The scope of an extracted execution also affects interpretation. A shared resource
connects several rotations, so an expanded graph can include events outside the
focal turnaround. We use one-to-one milestone pairs to calculate block duration and
26

inspect the graph separately. This prevents a long expanded execution from being
reported as a measured turnaround interval.
5.5 Relation to the Trace-Reconstruction Study
Tkacenko’s evaluation shows that traces selected using total turnaround bound-
aries can still disagree with observed internal activity durations [9, Section 6.2].
Our results identify a related limitation from another direction: a simulated median
close to an observed proxy median can coexist with a large mean discrepancy. In
both cases, an aggregate duration provides only limited evidence about the internal
process.
The evaluation settings are different. Tkacenko uses limited activity-level ground
truth and temporal acceptance filtering, whereas our current Berlin analysis has
no comparable internal service observations. Her state-space checks also concern
a time-neutralized model abstraction. Our completed-run checks do not reproduce
that analysis or establish the same properties. Keeping these differences explicit
avoids transferring validation claims between studies.
5.6 Remaining Work Before Stronger Conclusions
The next model version should correct local-time movement policy, resolve
aircraft-type candidates and distinguish operational turnarounds from long stays.
Evaluation should then use independently selected dates and matching observable
quantities, without forcing observed departures. RQ2 additionally requires termi-
nal/remote stratification and controlled delay-propagation experiments. RQ3 needs
broader input/capacity sensitivity and a declared operational or cost objective.
These tasks should precede claims of validated calibration or optimal allocation.
5.7 Summary
The completed pilot provides evidence about dependencies and assumption sen-
sitivity within the adapted model. Its main limits concern missing service observa-
tions, incomplete operational context and the inherited policy. Further work should
address these limits before extending the findings to actual BER operations. The

Chapter 6
Conclusion
Aircraft turnaround involves concurrent activities and shared resources, but the
available observations describe only part of this process. This thesis examines how
those observations can support an object-centric simulation while keeping missing
information explicit. We adapt the supplied extended airport model, prepare daily
Berlin inputs and analyze the generated logs in Promenade.
For RQ1, the work demonstrates how to construct finite demand from candidate
aircraft-linked intervals and retain the source and meaning of each input. It also
identifies the limit of the available data: internal service durations cannot be re-
covered from the current boundary proxies alone. Literature-based timing functions
therefore remain assumptions rather than fitted BER estimates.
For RQ2, the analysis shows how local waiting and parallel completion can lead
to different conclusions. Fuel dispatch has a visible queue, while cabin and boarding
work are usually last ready at the handling join. Shared-resource relationships also
show why an expanded object-centric execution should not automatically be treated
as one turnaround interval. A controlled terminal-versus-remote comparison and a
quantified delay-propagation experiment remain incomplete.
For RQ3, the controlled pilot compares six scenarios on two dates with five runs
per scenario and date. All 60 runs complete, producing 15,270 simulated executions
from 509 reused empirical interval cases. With the inherited curfew helper disabled,
literature timings shorten mean simulated block duration by 7.53 and 7.04 minutes.
Increasing fuel capacity greatly reduces dispatch waiting, while the corresponding
overall changes are only 0.62 and 0.22 minutes. These results demonstrate a method
for conditional resource evaluation, rather than an optimal actual staffing configu-

ration.
The policy comparison also reveals a confirmed UTC/local-time mismatch and
a strong interaction between timing assumptions and inherited policy. This finding
limits the interpretation of earlier differences and identifies a concrete requirement
for the next model version.
6.1 Future Work
The next steps are to correct the local-time movement policy, separate opera-
tional turnarounds from long stays and resolve conflicting aircraft-type candidates.
Empirical evaluation should then compare quantities with matching timestamp def-
initions on independently selected dates. Stand-specific and delay-propagation ex-
periments are needed to complete RQ2, while broader uncertainty analysis and a
declared allocation objective are needed for stronger RQ3 conclusions.
The completed work provides a traceable simulation and analysis procedure un-
der limited observations. Its findings are useful for examining the modeled system
and identifying assumptions that require further investigation. They do not yet es-
tablish a validated representation of BER ground handling.
